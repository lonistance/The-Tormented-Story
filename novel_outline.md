# The Tormented (The Condemned) — Full-Length Novel Detailed Outline

> Expanded from *Story Setting*. All original settings are retained; names, places, objects, and plot details newly added in this outline are marked 【tentative】 and may be adjusted at any time.
> The whole work spans seven volumes, 102 chapters. Each chapter provides: a one-line synopsis, scene expansion, key lines, chapter twist, foreshadow/callback, and writing notes.

---

## I. Overall Outline of the Work

### 1. One-Sentence Story

A half-blood child born and branded a "calamity" spends his life proving he is not a monster — from a soldier yearning for recognition, to the Death God forgotten by the world, to a husband and father who put down his sword; when he finally understands what power is for, he has lost everything he most wanted to protect, and at last makes his final bargain with the mysterious Neow and steps into the Spire.

### 2. Seven-Volume Structure Table

| Vol | Title | Time | Age | Core Theme | From → To |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | Blood-Stained Child | Birth ~12 | 0-12 | Why an unaccepted child craves power | Born & abandoned → Leaves home |
| II | Sword-Bearing Child | 12~18 | 12-18 | How a good person unknowingly cultivates a wrong answer | Meets knight → Leaves knight |
| III | War Child | 18~25 | 18-25 | One who wants to prove he is not a monster becomes a monster | Enters war → First failed challenge of a god |
| IV | Defying Gods, Death God Born | 25~40 | 25-40 | Gaining everything still cannot fill the hollow inside | Second challenge → Arm severed → Curse → Death God era |
| V | Man Who Forgot the Sword | 40+ ~ decades | — | A legend tries to become a person again | Puts down sword → Ordinary family life |
| VI | Man Who Lost Home | Happiness Ends | — | If I fail to protect, can I forgive myself | War arrives → Home destroyed → Truth |
| VII | The Last Bargain | Despair's End | — | Whether he is willing to take up the sword again | Meets Neow → Bargain → Enters the Spire |

### 3. Protagonist's Character Arc (Six Stages)

1. The abandoned child — "As long as I'm strong enough, I won't be abandoned."
2. The warrior seeking recognition — "As long as I win, everyone will accept me."
3. The Death God — "As long as I have enough power, all problems can be solved."
4. The ordinary man — "Maybe I don't need to prove myself."
5. Loss of everything — "Avoiding responsibility is also a kind of harm."
6. The Condemned — "I cannot change the past, but I can bear the future."

### 4. Motifs and Threads Through the Whole Book

- **The Mark**: The dark-red birthmark, from "proof of calamity" to "the Death God's mark," to the wife's "does it hurt?" It is the definition the world imposes on him, and the starting point of his final self-acceptance.
- **The Prophecy**: "When the red mark appears on the half-blood child, calamity will follow him." — The prophecy never said he would destroy the tribe; the truth is "he will witness the end of an era"; the root of the tragedy is not the prophecy but prejudice.
- **The Sword**: The knight's sword (protection) → Kael's sword (proof) → the Death God's sword (identity) → the sword that splits firewood (the process of putting it down) → ultimately "the sword is not for winning" (inheritance). The sword's imagery completes the semantic shift from "power" to "guardianship."
- **The Name**: Kael (mother/wife's call) → Red-Mark Child (the battlefield's name for him) → The Deathbringer ("Death God", the enemies' and the world's name) → The Condemned (self-naming). Every naming is a "being defined" or a "self-definition."
- **Leaving and Returning**: Vol I leaves the tribe (to grow strong); Vol II leaves the knight (to prove himself); Vol V leaves the battlefield (for peace); Vol VI leaves the village (for protection — the most fatal); Vol VII returns too late (The Man Who Returned Too Late).
- **The Necklace**: The plain necklace Kael made with his own hands, which his wife always wore — "this one is more like you." It is the proof that "she loved Kael, not the Death God."
- **The Curse**: Immortality. On the surface a gift, in truth "making you face loss forever." In the end it becomes the bargaining chip in his deal with Neow.
- **"You deserve to exist"**: The knight could not say it, the wife said it in his place, the child proved it, and finally he confirms it himself. This is the emotional terminus of the whole book.

### 5. Worldbuilding Supplements 【tentative】

- **Cinderstone Tribe**: A human-dominant border tribe that worships ancient gods and practices "blood-and-fire divination." Kael's mother is a dark elf who fled there years ago due to war; the tribe took her in with a dual attitude of "pity and wariness."
- **Humans & Dark Elves**: Tense relations between the two races; the half-blood is taboo. Human strength/endurance and dark-elf agility/night-vision — Kael's bloodline is the source of his talent and his original sin.
- **War God** (tentative name: Herrag【tentative】): Governs war itself. He does not remember people by strength; He only remembers mortals who dared stand in His path — in ten million years He has remembered only one (the weakest, Erian); He despises the clamor of those who come to prove themselves, and is the opponent and mirror of Kael's first half of life. He does not hate victors; He hates those who win yet do not understand what winning means — when Kael severs His arm, His anger is never from defeat, but from the fact that a mortal won and still does not know what victory means.
- **The Blight**: A calamity that drives people mad and mutates them, keeps the dead from resting, and originates from the Spire. It connects to the corrupted-world setting of *Slay the Spire*.
- **The Spire & Neow**: The link to the game itself. Neow is an observer, a trader, and a part of the disaster — she needs "a warrior who cannot die."

### 6. Overall Creative Principles

- Every volume has a "volume theme sentence" (per original setting).
- Every chapter must have "one scene, one key line, one emotional turn."
- Tragedy is written as "misunderstanding" (Kael always hears a correct sentence wrong); comedy as "clumsiness" (a legend who can't learn ordinary life).
- War scenes are restrained: no heroic glorification, only exhaustion, fear, hunger, death.
- Key rule: **the reader always knows a little more than Kael** (the prophecy's truth, the knight's past, the curse's true meaning), creating sustained irony and pity.

## II. Volume I: Blood-Stained Child (Birth ~12)

### Volume Overview

- Time: birth ~ about 12 years old
- Theme: why a child craves power
- Volume theme sentence: "When he was born, people feared his power. So he spent a life proving he deserved power. Only later did he understand: what people truly feared was never power."
- End-of-volume state: He believes "the world rejects the weak, so I must grow strong"; he does not know that what truly rejects him is fear.

### Chapter 1 An Ill-Omened Birth

- One line: A child who should not have been born is born just as the prophecy arrives.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Opening: deep in the night, the tribe's birth hut. Hearth fire, the women. The mother (a dark elf) is in labor; the father (a human) waits outside. A rainy night.
  2. The baby is born bearing a dark-red mark — like a burning brand. His first cry.
  3. The tribe's priest interprets the prophecy: "When the red mark appears on the half-blood child, calamity will follow him." The crowd goes silent; fear seeps in through the doorway.
  4. The mother's perspective: she holds the child tight and lets no one touch him. The father's perspective: he knows the tribe will not let this child be, but he is powerless.
- Key lines: Priest: "This is the god warning us." / Mother: "He is just a child."
- Chapter twist: After the first cry, the birth hut changes from joy to silence.
- Emotional arc: Expectation → shock → fear. From the first page the reader knows: this child's fate has already been defined.
- Foreshadow/callback: The shape of the birthmark (runs through the whole book); the prophecy's original text (true meaning revealed in Vol VII); the mother's identity.
- Writing notes: Don't write the grand spectacle of "calamity descends"; write the small things — the shadow of torches, the footsteps of women stepping back, no one daring to hold the child again.

### Chapter 2 The Child Feared

- One line: The clansmen feared him before they knew him.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Infancy: the mother is shunned, the father quarrels with the clansmen; the mother soothes the child with dark-elf lullabies.
  2. Learning to walk: children are pulled away by adults, "don't go near him."
  3. First called "child of calamity": at the village gate, a child's tactlessness, the adults' tacit consent.
  4. The mother makes him an amulet and hides it in his swaddling clothes — the first proof of "being loved."
- Key lines: A child: "Why are you different from us?" Kael (young): "I... don't know." (He does not know this question will repeat for a lifetime.)
- Chapter twist: Kael first realizes "I am different."
- Foreshadow/callback: The amulet (emotionally mirrored by the wife's necklace in Vol V); "Why are you different from us" is asked by his child in Vol V — the same question, a different answer.
- Writing notes: Coldness is more frightening than malice. Don't write beatings; write "the seat that is left empty for him."

### Chapter 3 The Frail Child

- One line: The monster of legend is actually an ordinary sickly child.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Frail: fever, coughing, the mother watches over him all night; the father injures himself gathering herbs.
  2. Play: children his age run and climb trees; he watches from the window. His mother teaches him herbs and stars.
  3. One "proof": he gathers courage to join the game and is deliberately left behind; he can't run fast enough, scrapes his knee, no one looks back.
  4. His mother asks if it hurts; he shakes his head — his first lesson: "not crying means fewer hurts."
- Key lines: Mother: "You don't need to be like them." (Kael: "Then what do you need me to be?") Mother: "Live, and then come home."
- Chapter twist: Kael learns to hide weakness. This becomes the foundation of all his later tragedy.
- Foreshadow/callback: The "frail child" contrasts with Vol IV's "monster beyond human limits" — his growth is not talent paying off, but being driven out by fear.
- Writing notes: This is the softest chapter of the volume, building strength for the tragedy to come.

### Chapter 4 The First Sign of Talent

- One line: His deed of proving he is not a monster becomes the evidence others use to call him a monster.
- Scene expansion:
  1. A beast attacks the village: deep night, wolves rush into the tribe, adults panic, children wail.
  2. Kael grabs a wooden stick and stands in front of several children — not courage, but instinct born of terror pushed to its limit.
  3. Combat instinct explodes: agility, strength, intuition utterly unlike a frail child. He drives off the beasts and collapses himself.
  4. Waking: no cheers. Everyone looks at him with an "as expected" face.
  5. His mother holds him and cries — not from joy, but from fear.
- Key lines: Priest: "As expected. The calamity has power." / Kael (weakly): "I... protected them." No one answers.
- Chapter twist: The first time his "power is seen," it pushes everyone further away.
- Foreshadow/callback: The "red mark" is first stained with the color of blood; the instinct to "protect others" and the consequence of "being feared" will be entangled for a lifetime.
- Writing notes: The combat scene is restrained; the focus is the crowd's silence after the fight.

### Chapter 5 Yearning for Recognition

- One line: He begins training not to become a hero, but to hear "you are not a calamity."
- Scene expansion:
  1. From then on Kael secretly trains: practicing with branches, learning tracking from hunters, practicing balance at night.
  2. The clansmen notice his progress, and their distance deepens further — every growth makes them fear him more.
  3. His mother tries to stop him: "Why must you prove it?" Kael: "Because I don't want them to fear me."
  4. The cycle is set: loneliness → training → strength → more loneliness.
- Key lines: Kael: "As long as I'm strong enough, they'll know I'm not a calamity." (The reader knows: they won't.)
- Chapter twist: Kael internalizes "growing strong" as the only cure.
- Foreshadow/callback: His training methods contrast with the knight's teaching in Vol II — his wild-grown "strength" lacks the "why."
- Writing notes: Use montage for the passing years: seasons turn, a branch becomes a wooden sword, a wooden sword becomes an iron one.

### Chapter 6 The Misunderstanding of the Prophecy

- One line: No formal exile — only an indifference no one takes responsibility for.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Young Kael overhears the clansmen in council: "He cannot stay." "Who will tell him?" — no one dares take responsibility.
  2. Then sustained coldness: they don't call his name, don't sit at his table, hint that he should leave.
  3. Kael asks: "What did I do wrong?" No one answers. He slowly understands: they don't hate him; they fear him.
  4. He makes the first great choice of his life: "If you fear who I am now, then I'll become someone you cannot ignore."
- Key lines: Kael: "What did I do wrong?" / Father (after a long silence): "You did nothing wrong." — The father's only and last direct look at him.
- Chapter twist: From "being rejected" to "deciding to leave," agency returns to his hands for the first time — but the direction is wrong.
- Foreshadow/callback: "You fear who I am now" is in dialogue with Vol VI's "he thought leaving would protect."
- Writing notes: The father's "you did nothing wrong" is the tear point of this chapter — right words, at the wrong time, still unable to change fate.

### Chapter 7 Leaving Home

- One line: He leaves without hatred, only a "wait for me to return."
- Scene expansion:
  1. At dawn he leaves with a small bundle (his mother's amulet, an old iron tool).
  2. His mother doesn't see him off — she cries behind the door, only stuffing dried rations into his pack.
  3. At the village gate, he looks back at the tribe and says: "Wait for me to return. When I'm strong enough, you'll know I'm not a calamity."
  4. No one responds. He walks into the wilderness through the morning mist.
- Key lines: Kael: "Wait for me to return." / Narrator (voice-over): "It took him many years to understand: the one he was waiting for had always been himself."
- Chapter twist: End of Vol I — he leaves, yet packs the obsession with "being accepted" into his bundle.
- Foreshadow/callback: This "wait for me to return" comes true in the cruelest way in Vol VI — he returns, but too late.
- Writing notes: Don't end with a tragic silhouette; write that after he walks far away, the people of the tribe breathe a sigh of relief — that is the real cruelty.

## III. Volume II: Sword-Bearing Child (12~18)

### Volume Overview

- Time: about 12~18 years old
- Core character: the wandering knight Adrian Vale
- Theme: how a good person unknowingly cultivates a wrong answer
- Volume theme sentence: "The knight gave him a sword. He thought it was the answer. But what the knight truly wanted to give him was: knowing who he is after putting the sword down."
- End-of-volume state: He gains skill, courage, and the foundation of goodness; he loses the right understanding of power. Wrong belief: "As long as I'm strong enough, I won't be abandoned again."

### Chapter 1 A Stranger from Afar

- One line: Killing a beast and killing a man are two completely different things.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael has wandered the wilderness alone for about half a year, sleeping rough, living by hunting beasts.
  2. Cornered by bandits: the first time he faces real "human enemies." His body is strong, but his experience is lacking — he hesitates.
  3. The price of hesitation: injury. A weary stranger of a knight appears, without a grand entrance.
  4. The knight says: "Child, that's not how you hold a sword." Then cleanly ends the fight — not killing, only disarming.
- Key lines: Kael: "They're enemies!" Knight: "They are men with no way out."
- Chapter twist: Kael sees another form of "strength" for the first time — strength with restraint.
- Foreshadow/callback: The knight's "no killing" handling is exactly the same as Vol V's Kael "subduing and releasing the bandits" — the inheritance begins in the first lesson.
- Writing notes: The action scene is short and precise; the focus is Kael's "hand gripping the sword trembling for the first time."

### Chapter 2 The Knight Saw No Monster

- One line: The first time in Kael's life — someone saw him without fear.
- Scene expansion:
  1. By the campfire, Kael bandages his wound, watching the knight warily.
  2. The knight notices the mark on his chest but doesn't step back.
  3. Kael asks provocatively: "You're not afraid of me?" "Why would I be?" "Because of this." He points at the mark.
  4. The knight glances at it: "I've seen things more frightening than a mark." A pause. "Like a man who believes the only thing he can become is a monster."
  5. Kael doesn't understand — he only remembers the sentence.
- Key lines: Knight: "Like a man who believes the only thing he can become is a monster."
- Chapter twist: Someone sees through his self-definition for the first time — and he can't understand it.
- Foreshadow/callback: "Believing he can only become a monster" is exactly who Kael becomes in Vol VI; this belated understanding doesn't come full circle until Vol VII.
- Writing notes: Let the dialogue rest in silence, no explanations. The reader understands now; Kael doesn't yet.

### Chapter 3 The Knight Takes Him In

- One line: Someone helps him simply because he is a child.
- Scene expansion:
  1. The knight sees Kael has nowhere to go, makes no plan, only asks: "If you want, you can travel with me."
  2. Kael asks "why are you helping me," the knight answers "because you need help" — so simple.
  3. Kael cannot understand this simplicity: in his life, all help came with conditions (strength, prophecy, value).
  4. He can never find a word for him — "knight" is too distant, "father" only ever said once in his heart; at the stream he wants to call out something, but the words die in his throat (the absence of a title, mirroring the absence of a name).
- Key lines: Kael: "Everyone who helps me does so because I might be useful." Knight: "Then you're useless now — why am I still here?"
- Chapter twist: Kael has a substitute for "family" for the first time.
- Foreshadow/callback: "Being together for no reason" becomes the prototype of the Vol V marriage.
- Writing notes: Adoption needs no ceremony; write the backs of two "loners" walking side by side.

### Chapter 4 The Knight in Kael's Eyes

- One line: The knight he saw was the right man, but the wrong version.
- Scene expansion:
  1. On the road, the knight helps villagers, travelers, people touched by war; he asks no payment.
  2. Kael observes: strong, calm, fearless, able to protect others.
  3. Kael's conclusion: the knight's power comes from "the sword." "If I had such power, no one would fear me."
  4. The narrator points out: the knight's true power is not the sword, but "having power and choosing restraint."
- Key lines: Kael: "One day I'll be as strong as you." Knight (doesn't answer, only thinks): "I hope you won't be as strong as me."
- Chapter twist: The mentor-student bond is set, but the divergence in understanding is planted.
- Foreshadow/callback: "Restraint" is a word repeated through the book — when Kael speaks his insulting words after severing the god's arm in Vol IV, it is exactly the moment he abandons restraint.
- Writing notes: Use abundant details to write the knight's "daily goodness," building emotional reserves for the tragedy to come.

### Chapter 5 A Day Without the Sword (Inserted Chapter, No Combat)

- One line: He didn't need to draw his sword to earn everyone's respect — Kael saw "another kind of strength" with his own eyes for the first time, and didn't recognize it as the days he would spend the rest of his life trying to return to.
- Scene expansion:
  1. After autumn harvest in Willowbrook Village (a small western-fantasy village: mill, barn, drying ground), the knight stays to do labor: fixing wooden-shingle roofs (the Gareth house), carrying water (for Hobbs the miller), picking beans (for Martha the widow), soothing children — all "work needing no skill," days passing without touching a sword.
  2. The knight treats daily life as a lesson, polishing his temperament: "When the water bucket sways, your footing is unsteady. Steady the heels, and the sword's root is steady." "Beans are picked one by one, the sword practiced stroke by stroke. If you want speed, first learn slowness." Kael only takes it as delays to his training, counting the days until they hit the road.
  3. The villagers respect him: keeping supper for him, gifting ale, pressing dried fruit on him; Kael does the same work and gets "that kid's diligent" — but not "impressive." What he wants is recognition, not help.
  4. A boy taunts him with "the old man with no name" and "you don't have a name either, do you?"; Kael's hand trembles on the sword with rage; the knight: "The hand. Trembling again."
  5. Martha invites them to stay for the winter ("that woodshed out back — cleaned up, a person can live in it") — Kael never treats it as a "choice" at all.
- Key lines: Kael: "I've trained so long — why does everyone praise you?" Knight: "First carry today's water steadily, then ask that."
- Chapter twist: The first divergence — Kael counts respect as "interest on the sword"; the knight teaches "living a life," the child learns "practicing martial arts" — one lesson, two ways of hearing it. The knight's silent tragedy: "I hope you won't be like me." He believes he can pave the "road that needs no sword" beneath the child's feet.
- Foreshadow/callback: a) "The hand. Trembling again." appears a second time (from the trembling of sword practice to the trembling of rage — he never learns "not to be moved"); b) the invitation of the woodshed and hot soup — an early echo of Vol V's "seeking an ordinary life": such days had been offered to him long before; he didn't hear it then; c) the knight waits to teach him "respect doesn't come that way," and waits a lifetime (connected to "the knight's greatest mistake").
- Writing notes: A whole chapter with no combat. Actions write "work," not "technique"; the calmer and closer to ordinary life, the more heartbreaking when the reader knows what follows — this is the life he could have chosen, and the one he finally walked away from.

### Chapter 6 The Knight's Secret Past

- One line: A man who never talks about the past is usually talked about by the past the most.
- Scene expansion:
  1. The knight never speaks of his past. Villagers have various rumors: battlefield hero / killer of a king / lost everything through defeat.
  2. Kael presses, the knight changes the subject; Kael learns not to ask.
  3. One detail: at night the knight grips his sword as if sleepwalking, waking with his hand soaked in cold sweat.
  4. Kael realizes for the first time: strong people also have things they fear.
- Key lines: Kael: "You have things you fear too?" Knight: "I fear." (Pause.) "So I hold the sword steadier."
- Chapter twist: Kael misreads it as "fear means grow stronger" — what the knight means is "fear, and still move forward."
- Foreshadow/callback: The knight's nightmare is replicated generationally in Vol V's Kael.
- Writing notes: Plenty of rumors, hidden truth; do not reveal the knight's past this chapter, only plant the leads.

### Chapter 7 The Knight's Tragedy

- One line: He once believed that once you defeated your enemies, peace would come.
- Scene expansion:
  1. (Retold by a storyteller) The young Adrian Vale: glory, idealism, firm belief that "evil must be eliminated."
  2. He takes part in a war, slays the enemy general, the war ends — peace lasts only one winter.
  3. New hatred is born: the dead's families, survivors' vengeance, new wars.
  4. In one operation, innocents die — possibly including people he had protected. Details are not public; only rumor remains.
- Key lines: a) (a rumored line) "He won the war, but lost his own name." b) From the knight's own mouth: "Adrian Vale was a fool — at least he used to be." (He believed that killing the wicked would make wickedness disappear.)
- Chapter twist: The reader, along with the storyteller, "hears" the knight's past; Kael still doesn't know — the information gap widens.
- Foreshadow/callback: **True-Name Rule** — this chapter presents the true name "Adrian Vale" (in the storyteller's tale), but the text never reveals that "the knight on the road" is that man; this foreshadow is only pointed out in Chapter 13 *Parting* when master and student go their separate ways; before then, no narration may let the reader connect the name to the knight (no confirming details, no appearance comparison, no narrator's asides identifying him). Also: this story is a rehearsal of Kael's Vols III-IV life — the two men are two eras of the same road.
- Writing notes: Use the "storyteller tells a tale" frame, half truth half fiction; the truth is left for Kael himself to piece together before Vol VII. The knight's reaction to the story must be restrained to nearly zero; the only crack is that word "fool."

### Chapter 8 The Knight's Awakening

- One line: He put down the sword, but did not forgive himself.
- Scene expansion:
  1. (Presented under the no-reveal rule) Reflection and awakening are voiced **through a third party commenting on the "stranger" Adrian Vale** — the knight's entire self-examination speaks of him as "he" instead of "I," with no self-confirmation using the word "I" anywhere in the text; Kael only takes it as hearing a deeper sequel to the story.
  2. He refuses the mayor's "stay" invitation — the awakening shows in behavior, not confession (Kael doesn't understand: "that's what everyone wants").
  3. A veteran at the way-station recognizes his hands as war-hardened hands, presses "which side," he answers beside the question — avoidance shown through action.
  4. The cart stuck in mud, a rope: the positive form of his awakening — "Sometimes a sword is worth less than a rope." "I teach you the sword so that one day you won't need it."
  5. Deep in the night, he whets the sword like an apology, and lets out a very soft "sorry" — he put down the sword; he did not forgive himself.
- Key lines: a) Knight (speaking of Adrian): "It began as atonement. When I couldn't pay it off, it became running." b) (the core lesson) "I teach you the sword so that one day you won't need it." c) Reserved outline line — "I left not because I wanted to go, but because I didn't dare to stop." — this sentence is deferred to the parting between teacher and student.
- Chapter twist: Kael misreads the knight's "refusing to stay" as "stability is a cage, glory is a bad thing" — he sets his ambition in reverse: he wants every recognition anyone can give (another missed chance).
- Foreshadow/callback: "Avoidance" becomes Kael's core flaw in Vol VI — the disciple inherits the master's solution and the master's disease; "I left not because I wanted to go, but because I didn't dare to stop" is kept for the parting chapter; read the two passages together.
- Writing notes: An inner-monologue chapter that doesn't use inner monologue — use the three layers of "commenting on others, behavior, objects" as indirect description; any occurrence of "back when I..." or "I once..." violates the rule; the reveal holds until the parting chapter.

### Chapter 9 The Knight Teaches Kael

- One line: "You've already won." "But I can still get stronger."
- Scene expansion:
  1. Training begins: basics, footwork, defense, calm.
  2. Kael's talent is startling; his progress far exceeds expectations — the knight first realizes this child may become a great warrior.
  3. The knight also sees the danger: Kael's training always pursues faster, stronger, more victory.
  4. The knight calls a stop: "You've already won." Kael: "But I can still get stronger." The knight grows silent — he has heard his younger self.
- Key lines: Knight (inwardly): "What can I teach him so that he won't become me?"
- Chapter twist: The knight senses the problem but doesn't know how to say it — groundwork for Chapter 9's "greatest mistake."
- Foreshadow/callback: The sentence "You've already won" is only truly learned in Vol VI's father-daughter duel, when Kael says "Your sword is sharp. But it sets my heart at ease."
- Writing notes: Write the training scenes as a parallel montage of "sword lessons" and "life lessons."

### Chapter 10 The Knight's Greatest Mistake

- One line: He loved him with actions, but never told him with words.
- Scene expansion:
  1. The knight knows what Kael truly needs is "understanding power," but he cannot express it.
  2. He is used to acting: shielding Kael from danger, giving him food, mending his clothes, keeping silent watch at night.
  3. He rarely says "you are already enough" — the sentence was stuck in his throat for decades.
  4. Kael's understanding: "You never praise me. So I'm not good enough yet." The root of the tragedy is formally set.
- Key lines: Kael (inwardly): "You never praise me. So I'm not good enough yet."
- Chapter twist: Love and understanding misalign — both love each other, both misunderstand each other.
- Foreshadow/callback: In Vol V, Kael learns to tell his child "you are already enough" — he says for the knight the sentence the knight never said. One of the most important emotional closures of the book.
- Writing notes: This chapter demands great skill in "leaving blanks" — the reader must see the knight's love while understanding Kael's misunderstanding.

### Chapter 11 The Child's Question

- One line: "If I'm strong enough, will they accept me?" — A question he never once asked right in his whole life.
- Scene expansion:
  1. One night, Kael asks the knight this question.
  2. The knight wants to answer "yes," but he knows it's not certain. He is silent for a long time.
  3. The knight says: "What truly matters is that you accept yourself."
  4. Kael hears it as "don't care what others think," yet inside he still longs for others' acknowledgment.
- Key lines: Knight: "What truly matters is that you accept yourself."
- Chapter twist: The right answer is given for the first time, misheard for the first time.
- Foreshadow/callback: In Vol VII, when Kael says this sentence to his child (the child in his memory), he finally understands it.
- Writing notes: This is the model chapter of the book's "misaligned dialogue."

### Chapter 12 The Allure of War

- One line: The battlefield means to Kael a place where he can prove himself.
- Scene expansion:
  1. War breaks out at the border; Kael sees real soldiers for the first time, hears the legends of war.
  2. Soldiers who hear of his deeds say: "A child like this should be on the battlefield."
  3. Kael's heart stirs: the battlefield = a place to prove himself. He asks the knight to come along; the knight objects.
  4. Knight: "That's no place for a child." Kael: "I'm not a child anymore."
- Key lines: Knight: "That's precisely because you're still young." (This sentence becomes the fuse of their separation.)
- Chapter twist: The first fundamental disagreement between Kael and the knight.
- Foreshadow/callback: "That's precisely because you're still young" — when Kael later tells his child "to leave, you must first defeat me," he finally understands what the knight felt that year.
- Writing notes: Writing Kael's excitement, let the reader also see the other side of war — this is "irony of foreknowledge."

### Chapter 13 Kael Leaves the Knight

- One line: This is not a quarrel; it is a sorrowful farewell.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael decides to leave. The conversation between the two is calm and painful.
  2. Kael: "You taught me to hold the sword. Why do you stop me from using it now?" Knight: "Because the sword is not the way you prove yourself." "Then what is?" "Protection."
  3. What Kael hears: "You're not strong enough yet."
  4. Kael: "I'll prove it to you." The knight does not stop him — he knows some answers can only be found alone.
- Key lines: Knight: "The sword is not the way you prove yourself." (What Kael hears: "You're not strong enough yet.")
- Chapter twist: Teacher and student part; the misunderstanding is frozen.
- Foreshadow/callback: "I'll prove it to you" runs through Vol IV, until after the father-daughter duel of Vol VI Kael understands the true meaning of "protection."
- Writing notes: The first "tear-jerker chapter" of the book — write it with restraint.

### Chapter 14 Parting

- One line: "If one day you're tired, come back." — He needed decades to understand this sentence.
- Scene expansion:
  1. The knight gives him a sword — not his own blade, an ordinary sword.
  2. Knight: "What truly matters isn't the sword." Kael: "I'll make it a legend." The knight falls silent — that sentence is dangerous.
  3. The last embrace. Knight: "If one day you're tired, come back." Kael: "I won't fail." Knight: "That's not what I meant."
  4. Kael walks away. The knight stands in place watching his back for a long time.
- Key lines: Knight: "That's not what I meant." (But Kael doesn't look back.)
- Chapter twist: End of Vol II. Kael walks toward the battlefield bearing skill and misunderstanding.
- Foreshadow/callback: The fate of that "ordinary sword" — in Vol V it is used to "split firewood" (unsuccessfully), in Vol VI it is drawn again to protect the village. It has always been a "non-legendary sword," just as Kael's original identity.
- Writing notes: Closing image: the knight watches him go, the old horse snorts, the cloak is lifted by the wind. No tears written.

## IV. Volume III: War Child (18~25)

### Volume Overview

- Time: about 18~25 years old
- Core character: the veteran Erian Sol — an ordinary man dragged into war. He has no grand ideals and is no idealist; his homeland was destroyed by war, and he joined the army for one reason only: to fill his stomach. As a victim of war, he has witnessed countless deaths and the dehumanization war causes; among everyone Kael knows, he is the only one who saw through the nature of war and was never brainwashed. He possesses nearly all of an ordinary man's flaws: he fears death, and he deserted once; but he never betrayed his humanity — he prioritizes saving the wounded, even "enemies," and refuses senseless slaughter. He is the antithesis of the battlefield "Death God" Kael: "the ordinary man surviving desperation."
- Contrast Foreshadow (the whole book): The War God's only remembered mortal is not Kael (strongest, fights without knowing why) but Erian (weakest, dares to stand against Him). — Hooked in Vol III Chs 12-13, paid off in Vol IV Ch 7 and Vol V Ch 2.
- Theme: a child who wants to prove he is not a monster eventually becomes the monster people speak of
- Volume theme sentence: "He heard everyone tell him the sword cannot bring peace. But he only heard one sentence: your sword is still not strong enough."
- End-of-volume state: He still holds goodness, but chooses to suppress it. Belief: "As long as I'm strong enough, I can prove everyone wrong."

### Chapter 1 Stepping onto the Battlefield (Day 1: Arrival, Before Dark)

- One line: The camp tells him for the first time — here no one asks who you are, only how strong you are. He believes it.
- Scene expansion:
  1. The opening starts from an action: he puts his belongings into a shared tent's footlocker — a sword, some clothes, a little money, the amulet his mother sewed. Someone behind asks: "New arrival?" He looks up. This is his first real entry into the adult world.
  2. Registration: a half-eared old veteran asks what he did; "hunter." The veteran sees the mark on his collar, doesn't pry, makes a note. It's the first time Kael doesn't hide the mark in public. Quartermaster Harvey (one-time character) issues bedding and rations, counting even a single grain. Kael first realizes: what keeps men alive in camp may not be the sword, but grain.
  3. The camp environment is only a "walking glance": chevaux-de-frise, mud, cooking smoke, laundry lines, the sound of whetting, gambling tables, ale barrels — everything has a person using it; no piling up of nouns.
  4. First supper (the chapter's core): black bread, thin soup, salt meat. He thinks it's the best meal he's ever had — not for the taste, but because for the first time he feels he belongs somewhere. The man beside him looks at his mark, pauses: "That Kael?" He didn't get "calamity" — the man asks: "Heard you can use a sword?" "I can." "Good." Then he goes back to eating.
  5. Erian appears: he snatches the last bowl of soup. "That's mine." "If you don't hurry, it'll be mine." He drinks it down, belches, wipes his mouth, and leaves. Someone in the tent curses with a laugh, "even the Long-Legged Doctor's grabbing soup" — Kael hears that nickname for the first time.
  6. First training (wooden swords): Bane (one-time character, an old veteran) tests him. Seeing how young he is, he deliberately underestimates him, moving slowly. Kael reads his center of gravity — in two or three moves, the wooden sword stops at his throat. Silence around them. "Who taught you?" "A knight." Someone whistles: "This kid's got something." He enjoys it immensely.
  7. Unfairness (very light): Orlan, a young knight in full armor (one-time character), watches from a distance: "Just a country trick." Kael wants a rematch. An officer stops it: "Enough." "Why?" "Because he's a knight, and you're not." Kael doesn't argue; he talks himself down: It's fine. Things will be different once we're on the battlefield.
  8. Night: the amulet, the knot on the sword's grip, the six years at the mill. He speaks to the tent roof: as long as I win, everyone will accept me.
- Key lines: Kael (to the tent roof): "As long as I win, everyone will accept me." / Soldier beside him: "Heard you can use a sword?" "I can." / Officer: "Because he's a knight, and you're not."
- Chapter twist: The first seed of hope is planted — the illusion of "the camp only looks at ability" takes shape.
- Foreshadow/callback: The obsession with "accept me" never changes, only the method; the quartermaster's ledger = war runs on grain; the "Long-Legged Doctor" nickname first appears; the mark is first treated as "can you fight."
- Writing notes: Lived-in atmosphere is the soul (split shoe soles, stolen needle-and-thread, cursing over lost dice, the cook driving off soldiers stealing meat, slipping meat to a friend) — all behavioral; the dual-layer narration relies only on selection of material, no narrator explanations; future voice 0 times.

### Chapter 2 War in Reality (Day 2: First Steps Past the Palisade)

- One line: He draws a real sword for the first time. No glory — only blood, weight, and a cry of "Moth...er...". More than enemy soldiers died — the knight by the drill ground yesterday became, today, a message relayed on the way back to camp.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Dawn formation: horns, mud-boots stamping through mud, armor clattering, the banner rising, the officer calling roll. Kael stands among several hundred men for the first time, feeling he belongs to something bigger. He thinks: as long as I'm strong enough, I can protect these people. The patrol leader is a young knight in bright armor — the one from the drill-ground edge last night. Kael remembers "just a country trick."
  2. The task: rotating guard at an outpost, with a patrol of a border village along the way (small-scale, not a great battle). Lived-in details along the road: men writing letters, boasting about "town women," arguing about where to drink after rotation. Bane is in the column too.
  3. The village is attacked (routed soldiers/bandits): the first real draw of the sword. A wooden sword and a real one are completely different — blood, weight, bone, sound. But he finds he isn't afraid; he is faster than the others. He is shaken.
  4. First kill: an enemy soldier about his age. The two grapple; his instinct wins. The enemy falls and, dying, cries: "Moth...er..." Kael's hand trembles, but he doesn't break.
  5. Rescue: on the retreat, a child (or a woman) is trapped while the others have withdrawn. Kael stays, kills the enemy, drags the person out. The person only sobs. He hands them to the rear — "I can protect people." This matters far more than "I can kill."
  6. Orlan's death (not spread out before Kael's eyes, author's decision): during the withdrawal there's the sound of a rear guard blocking behind them, and someone pulls him "don't stop." He glances back once — he only sees the bright armor at the crossroads. Back in camp, a comrade relays it (the author's call: Orlan isn't an arrogant braggart; he has a knight's code, and his sacrifice is justified — holding the rear to cover his comrades): Bane crouches by the fire and tells it, "He said he was a knight, and a knight had no business running behind the others." The tone of the retelling is flat, unheroic; "sharp tongue" is brushed over with one phrase, without condemnation. This must never be written as web-novel "provoking the protagonist and getting slapped down" — Orlan's death is a positive presentation of the knight's code, to be read alongside Chapter 1's "just a country trick."
  7. Return to camp at night: some praise him, some fear him (the talk of "wolf cub" — wariness, not praise), some envy him. Kael deliberately ignores the negative voices: "They just don't know me yet."
  8. At night: Erian shares his own water with the wounded and bandages an enemy soldier's wound. Kael sees it and doesn't ask. (Left in silence, unexplained.)
  9. Deep night, he presses his trembling hand onto his knee, pushing it down: "I'm not a monster. I just... won." His first self-deception. Then he thinks of Orlan: he tries to recall his face, tries for a long time, can't — he only remembers the bright armor. He gives himself a conclusion: "Strong men survive. I survived, so I'm strong enough." (Self-paralysis; the reader's side: yesterday's comrade became today's corpse; war's "fairness" lies not in "asks no origin," but in that regardless of origin everyone has only one life — presented through selected material, no narrator explanation.)
- Key lines: Enemy soldier (last words): "Moth...er..." / Kael (to his own hand): "I'm not a monster. I just... won." / Kael (thinking of Orlan): "Strong men survive. I survived, so I'm strong enough." / Bane's retelling: "He said he was a knight, and a knight had no business running behind the others."
- Chapter twist: The first crack opens between "victory" and "right" — he patches it that very night with his own explanation; Orlan's death drives one more nail into "the camp only looks at ability," and Kael numbs it away with "strong men survive" — the first counter-example, and he doesn't look.
- Foreshadow/callback: In Vol VI he recalls "the first kill" — the same image, a different answer; "I can protect people" = the phase of saving ordinary people; "wolf cub" = awe is not acceptance (expanded in Chapter 3); "can't recall the face, only the bright armor" — origin clearer than the man (the hidden fairness thesis is left to the reader).
- Writing notes: Don't write a heroic single-combat of generals; write "an enemy as young as you" — one of the most important war scenes of the whole book; Orlan's death is only given through the retelling (Kael has no direct view), flat tone, no melodrama, no heroism; Kael feels no empathy, no grief, only digests the news into self-deception; future voice 0 times.

### Chapter 3 Glory Brought by Talent (Day 3: Aftermath and Campfire)

- One line: He got what he had wanted since childhood — awe. He thought that was acceptance.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Daytime aftermath: Quartermaster Harvey makes an exception and gives him an extra piece of salt meat (the reader: because he's useful); tentmates buy him ale; whistles and "this kid's got something"; someone calls him "Red-Mark Child."
  2. Awe ≠ acceptance (light): someone wants to clap his shoulder, then pulls back; when he walks past, talk lowers. He takes it for respect.
  3. Afternoon, Kael: "This place is better than I'd thought." Erian: "What part?" "At least here people only look at ability." Erian laughs: "You really think so?" "Isn't it?" Erian looks at his sword: "When you've lived a few more years, you'll know." "What do you mean?" "Nothing much." Then he goes back to eating. (Restrained, no sermonizing.)
  4. Night, the campfire. Kael polishes his sword. Two veterans drink and talk a little way off: "Who goes to the outpost tomorrow?" "Me." "Didn't you just get back?" "No choice. They're short-handed there." "What about those villagers yesterday?" Silence. "Didn't make it." Kael looks up — he realizes for the first time that not everyone can be saved.
  5. He can't accept it: "If we're strong enough, we could have saved them." Erian sits beside him, silent for a while: "Maybe." He stands up. "Sleep early."
  6. Ending: Kael looks at his sword and thinks: I've finally found the place that belongs to me.
  7. Future voice (this chapter's only one) closes: He didn't know that in the future he would hear this sentence countless times — the sword cannot bring peace. And every time, the words he heard were another sentence: your sword is still not strong enough.
- Key lines: Veteran A: "What about those villagers yesterday?" Veteran B: "Didn't make it." / Kael: "If we're strong enough, we could have saved them." / Erian: "Maybe." "Sleep early."
- Chapter twist: Hope reaches its peak ("I've finally found the place that belongs to me") — while the reader already sees its foundation: labels, grain, origin, and death.
- Foreshadow/callback: The seed of the second name "Red-Mark Child" (echoing Vol I Ch 4's "Red-Mark Child" foreshadow); "awe ≠ acceptance" is developed across Chapter 4; "didn't make it" and Kael's obsession with "not being able to save everyone"; the volume theme sentence lands in full for the first time.
- Writing notes: Alternate-cut glory scenes with lonely details; the last night must be warm and quiet, letting the reader and Kael together believe "this place really can work" — the next chapter begins to tear it apart.

### Chapter 4 He Misunderstood Acceptance

- One line: When he was little, the clansmen feared him; now the enemy fears him — he considers this progress.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Time jumps to weeks later: victory after victory — night raids, breaking formations, slaying generals. "Red-Mark Child" becomes the soldiers' name for him (the seed of Chapter 3 grown).
  2. He is toasted, cheered, looked up to for the first time; at night he finds awe hasn't turned into closeness — no one dares clap his shoulder (Chapter 3's "light" magnified).
  3. An old soldier (talking behind his back): "Standing with him, I always feel the Death God's shadow right behind."
  4. Kael finds "fear" and "acknowledgment" are being conflated: the terror in captured enemy soldiers' eyes when they look at him is exactly the same as the clansmen's years ago.
  5. He deliberately doesn't look. Self-persuasion: the enemy fears me, so I'm strong; strength is acknowledgment. The tragic loop closes.
- Key lines: Kael: "They've finally acknowledged me." (The reader: they only fear his power, not him.)
- Chapter twist: He takes "awe" for "acceptance," tragedy for progress.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol IV's "They love the Death God, not Kael" is this chapter magnified; "Red-Mark Child" stands alongside "The Condemned" (Vol VII).
- Writing notes: No major events in this chapter — all interior drama plus small details; it is the volume's positioning anchor.

### Chapter 5 Do You Want to Die? (formerly "Meeting Erian")

- One line: "Do you want to die?" "I just want to win."
- Note: Erian's first appearance is already in Chapter 1's supper (grabbing soup), and the nickname "Long-Legged Doctor" already appears in Chapter 1; this chapter is no longer a first meeting, but the night when the two's "bond of life and death" takes shape.
- Scene expansion:
  1. In a pursuit operation, Kael breaks from the column and goes deep into enemy territory alone, nearly killed.
  2. The veteran Erian saves him — not out of loyalty, but habit: he has saved too many people to remember. He bandages calmly, and speaks calmly.
  3. Erian: "Do you want to die?" Kael: "I just want to win." Erian: "In war, those two are usually the same."
  4. The two become comrades-in-arms — Erian is the only man who dares tell him the truth.
- Key lines: Erian: "In war, those two are usually the same."
- Chapter twist: Kael meets his first "friend who speaks the truth."
- Foreshadow/callback: Erian and the knight form "two men who speak the truth"; Kael fails to understand both times. The habit of saving people regardless of friend or foe — the reason he stands before the surrendering soldiers in Chapter 8. In dialogue with Chs 1-2's soup-grabbing and bandaging: the friendship begins with "cursing each other."
- Writing notes: The friendship of two soldiers begins with "cursing"; write their understanding without needing words. Erian's eyes must be "like an ordinary man who has seen too many dead" — weary, not numb.

### Chapter 6 Erian Saw His Past

- One line: "You're not fighting for victory. You're proving something."
- Scene expansion:
  1. Erian observes: Kael doesn't fight like a soldier; he fights like a man looking for an answer.
  2. Erian says it straight: "You're not fighting for victory. You're proving something." Kael falls silent — he hit the mark.
  3. Kael lets down a little of his guard before another person for the first time, then immediately puts the armor back on.
- Key lines: Erian: "You're proving something." Kael: "Proving what?" Erian: "The word you dare not say."
- Chapter twist: Kael is completely seen through for the first time.
- Foreshadow/callback: "Proving" is the book's first verb, not replaced until Vol VII's "I cannot change the past, but I can bear the future."
- Writing notes: A dialogue chapter, restrained; let silence carry most of the emotion.

### Chapter 7 Erian's Past

- One line: "Every enemy I killed left a new enemy behind."
- Scene expansion:
  1. Erian tells his own past: not an idealist's remorse, but an ordinary man's ledger — his home was burned by war, his parents gone; he fled all the way to a recruiting camp and signed up for a bowl of rice. "I joined because the army feeds you."
  2. He has seen the true face of war: victory buys not peace but the next war; a friend died beside him; enemy soldiers died in ditches no one buried — when a man dies, there's no difference between them.
  3. Erian: "It took me ten years to understand that every enemy I killed left a new enemy behind." — This isn't a moral; he counted it with his own eyes.
  4. Kael doesn't accept it — he only understands the first half, "it took ten years": too long; I have to be faster.
- Key lines: Erian: "Every enemy I killed left a new enemy behind."
- Chapter twist: The second time "a right sentence is heard wrong."
- Foreshadow/callback: This sentence completes its echo in Vol VI as "You always choose war, so war follows you."
- Writing notes: Erian's telling must be plain, like an old soldier's murmur after drinking — no grievance, no tears, just bookkeeping.

### Chapter 8 The First Conflict

- One line: Kael wants to slaughter the surrendering soldiers; Erian stands in front of him.
- Scene expansion:
  1. After a battle, the enemy surrenders. Kael believes "as long as the enemy lives, the war continues" and prepares to act.
  2. Erian stops him: "They've already lost." Kael: "An enemy is an enemy." Erian: "Do you really believe that?"
  3. Erian: "You sound like a frightened man." Kael is furious — because it's true.
  4. Kael falls out with Erian for the first time, but in the end he doesn't act. He doesn't know why.
  5. Detail: when Erian stands in front of him, his hands are shaking and his voice is shaking too — he isn't fearless; he is afraid, and he still doesn't step aside. He has dodged conscription and dodged battle his whole life, but "watching surrendering men die before his eyes" is the line he cannot run from.
- Key lines: Erian: "You sound like a frightened man."
- Chapter twist: Kael's last brush with "the knight's way."
- Foreshadow/callback: Kael's eventual "not acting" — the remnant of goodness; Vol V's "subduing and releasing the bandits" is the fulfilled version of this moment. Erian deserted once — precisely because he ran, he knows which line he cannot run from.
- Writing notes: The conflict is intense but brief; after the anger comes silence. Erian's courage must be written as "blocking with trembling hands," not "chin held high."

### Chapter 9 The Knight's Words Return

- One line: "Your sword is strong. But you must know when not to use it." — He mishears again.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Erian: "Your sword is strong. But you must know when not to use it."
  2. Kael thinks of the knight — the two said the same thing. He is angry: why does everyone hold me back?
  3. He merges the two sentences into: "They think I'm not strong enough."
  4. The second misunderstanding is complete. From then on he stops asking anyone "why."
- Key lines: Kael (inwardly): "They're not protecting me; they don't believe in me."
- Chapter twist: Kael closes the channel of "listening," paving the way for Vol IV's self-destruction.
- Foreshadow/callback: "When not to use it" — Kael only truly masters it in Vol VI when he deflects his blade.
- Writing notes: This chapter is the confluence of misunderstandings; keep the structure clean.

### Chapter 10 The End of War

- One line: He wins honor, yet no one asks him if he's tired.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Years of fighting; Kael becomes a young legend: turning the tide of campaigns, honored by the king, invited by nobles, worshipped by soldiers.
  2. At the banquet: fine wine, panegyrics, toasts. He responds with smiles.
  3. Deep night, the feast ends; he walks back to his tent alone, no one walking with him.
  4. He remembers his childhood wish, then the fear on the clansmen's faces — those faces at the banquet are actually the same.
- Key lines: Noble: "Without you, we would have failed long ago." (No one asks: "Are you tired?")
- Chapter twist: Honor in hand, emptiness begins.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol IV's "hollow victory" is this chapter magnified; "no one asks if you're tired" contrasts with Vol V's wife asking "what are you afraid of."
- Writing notes: Write all the glitter of the banquet scene, all the loneliness of the night scene.

### Chapter 11 Erian's Departure

- One line: "You still have time. Because you haven't lost everything yet."
- Scene expansion:
  1. Erian leaves the army — for a perfectly ordinary reason: he joined for the food, and now that the war has come this far, he wants to go see if that patch of land back home is still there. "You're done fighting?" "I can't fight anymore. I want to go home and farm."
  2. Erian: "You still have time." Kael: "Time changes nothing." Erian: "It will. Because you haven't lost everything yet."
  3. Kael doesn't understand. The two part ways.
- Key lines: Erian: "Because you haven't lost everything yet."
- Chapter twist: The second "truth-teller" leaves. The foreshadow is formally hooked.
- Foreshadow/callback: Only after losing everything in Vol VI does Kael understand the weight of this sentence — "still have time" is a truth Erian verified with his whole life.
- Writing notes: The farewell scene is short, but make the reader remember Erian. His leaving has no ritual of awakening — he's just a man who, at last, no longer has to fight.

### Chapter 12 Erian Challenges the God

- One line: He has run his whole life. Only this time, he forgot to run.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Years later, rumors: Erian challenged the War God. The reason is unknown; rumors differ — some say he went mad, some say he wanted revenge.
  2. (Reader-perspective interlude) The truth: not glory, not power. The War God was about to crush the place he guarded — the wounded he had fought to save, the people he had spent the rest of his life watching over. He has hidden his whole life; this time, there's no hiding.
  3. Erian climbs the mountain alone and faces the god. The hand gripping the sword trembles — just as it did when he stood before the surrendering soldiers.
- Key lines: Erian (inwardly): "I'm afraid. But even afraid, I have to stand here."
- Chapter twist: The book's first "mortal challenges a god" — yet the challenger is a man known as a coward.
- Foreshadow/callback: Paving the way for Kael's challenge in Chapter 15 — the same act, the opposite motive; the contrast foreshadow is hooked: the War God's only remembered mortal is not Kael (strongest, fights without knowing why) but Erian (weakest, dares to stand against Him).
- Writing notes: Skim over the challenge itself; focus on the "why" — his "why" contains no grand philosophy.

### Chapter 13 The God Did Not Kill Him

- One line: "You knew you would lose." "The people I saved are over there."
- Scene expansion:
  1. The fight: Erian loses badly, but the God stops.
  2. God: "You knew you would lose." Erian: "I knew." God: "Then why did you come?" Erian: "Because the people I saved are over there. If you step over them, they die."
  3. The God looks at him — not for his character, but because in ten million years of life He has seen countless powerful men and, for the first time, a man who can't even hold his sword steady yet dares to stand in His path.
  4. Erian descends the mountain, goes back to farming, and is never seen again (becoming the Vol V foreshadow).
- Key lines: Erian: "I'm afraid. But the people I saved are over there." / God: "I remember you."
- Chapter twist: The God has never remembered power, but the act of standing in His path — Kael knows nothing of this.
- Foreshadow/callback: The contrast foreshadow formally lands: the War God's only remembered mortal is not Kael (strongest, fights without knowing why) but Erian (weakest, dares to stand against Him). Paid off in Vol IV Ch 7, Vol V Ch 2, and the Vol VII finale.
- Writing notes: The God's image makes its first formal appearance; mind the characterization — His acknowledgment is not generous; He has given it only a few times in His life.

### Chapter 14 Kael Hears of It

- One line: Everyone is saying "he lost"; only Kael hears "even he couldn't do it."
- Scene expansion:
  1. The news returns to camp: soldiers mock the old veteran for going mad.
  2. Kael digests the information alone; his understanding: even Erian couldn't overcome a god — I must become stronger.
  3. He decides to challenge the god. The surface reason is "proving my sword"; the true reason is "unable to accept being looked down on still."
- Key lines: Kael: "If even he lost, then I must become stronger."
- Chapter twist: Misreading another man's story, he decides on a self-destructive path.
- Foreshadow/callback: One of the book's most important "misreadings," leading directly to Vol IV's fall.
- Writing notes: Keep this chapter short, but give the decision its full weight.

### Chapter 15 The First Challenge of a God

- One line: He gives everything in the fight; the god doesn't even take it seriously.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael seeks the War God and meets Him at a battlefield ruin.
  2. The God looks at him the way one looks at a man whose name He won't remember — "Another man seeking glory." Kael: "I'm here to prove you wrong."
  3. The fight: Kael gives it everything, but the gap is a chasm. The god doesn't even take it seriously.
  4. Kael is struck down, utterly defeated. Before he falls he sees: there is no him in the god's eyes — His gaze passes over him, as if remembering someone else.
- Key lines: God: "Another man seeking glory."
- Chapter twist: Kael first faces "there is power above power" — and "there are things beyond power."
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol VII's road to the Spire is the terminus of this thread. The contrast foreshadow half-pays off: in the god's memory there is a man who sought no glory (Erian); the man before Him now, seeking glory, He does not remember.
- Writing notes: Write the fight as "despair," not "spectacle."

### Chapter 16 The God's Dismissal

- One line: "Your sword is sharp. But your heart is too loud."
- Scene expansion:
  1. The god doesn't kill him — not out of mercy, but contempt.
  2. God: "Your sword is sharp. But your heart is too loud."
  3. Kael falls in the dust, unable for the first time to prove himself with power.
  4. The god's back disappears. Kael lies there for a long time.
- Key lines: God: "Your sword is sharp. But your heart is too loud."
- Chapter twist: One of the book's most important judgments — it foreshadows the answers of Vols VI and VII.
- Foreshadow/callback: When Kael faces "what the sword cannot solve" in the Blight of Vol VII, this sentence echoes again.
- Writing notes: This line must be driven into the reader's heart like a nail.

### Chapter 17 The Choice After Defeat

- One line: "The sword isn't wrong. My sword just isn't strong enough."
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael reflects briefly: the knight, Erian, the first kill.
  2. He almost admits "they were right" — but admitting it means the past was meaningless.
  3. He chooses the other road: "The sword isn't wrong. My sword just isn't strong enough."
  4. End of Vol III: he stands up and walks toward the abyss of "stronger."
- Key lines: Kael: "The sword isn't wrong. My sword just isn't strong enough."
- Chapter twist: The book's greatest fork in the road — he picks the wrong branch, and the reader sees it.
- Foreshadow/callback: "Admitting the past was meaningless" is a question Vol VII forces him to face again.
- Writing notes: The ending must carry the pain of "knowing it's an abyss and jumping anyway."

## V. Volume IV: Defying Gods, Death God Born (25~40)

### Volume Overview

- Time: about 25~40 (the timeline blurs afterward because of immortality)
- Theme: he finally gains the power everyone dreams of, only to find he still cannot fill the hollow inside
- Volume theme sentence: "He spent a life proving he was not a calamity. In the end, everyone believed he was a hero. Only he himself knew he was nearly forgetting how to be a person."
- End-of-volume state: He has invincible power, a legendary name, eternal life; he loses his original heart, his human connections, his knowledge of himself.

### Chapter 1 The Young Man After Defeat

- One line: After defeat he doesn't fall immediately — he doubts for a long time first.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael leaves the battlefield and travels alone, thinking over the words of the knight / Erian / the god.
  2. He almost convinces himself "they were right," but cannot accept it: that would mean the deaths and pains of the past were all mistakes.
  3. He chooses to go on — not from confidence, but from not daring to look back.
- Key lines: Kael: "I can't be wrong. If I am, then everyone who died by my sword died for nothing."
- Chapter twist: Acting under the name of "responsibility," practicing "avoidance."
- Foreshadow/callback: In Vol VII he finally admits "I destroyed many things" — the same fact, two framings.
- Writing notes: Write the struggle in full, so the reader understands why he chooses the wrong road.

### Chapter 2 Seeking Power

- One line: He doesn't enjoy killing; he truly believes "strong enough ends war."
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael begins studying every means of enhancement: ancient martial arts, forbidden techniques, dark-elf bloodline secrets, enemy technology.
  2. His body stops being ordinary: reaction, senses, recovery far beyond human.
  3. He tells himself: this is to end war. The reader knows: this is to fill the hollow.
- Key lines: Kael: "Once I'm strong enough, I can end the war." (Narrator: a wrong goal can drive a man in exactly the wrong direction.)
- Chapter twist: The acquisition of power begins to alienate his body.
- Foreshadow/callback: The link between the dark-elf bloodline and the mark — the "red mark" begins to take on a concrete embodiment of power.
- Writing notes: Power writing must carry a "sense of cost": with every gain in strength, something is paid.

### Chapter 3 The Legend of the Battlefield

- One line: He isn't an army; he is a disaster.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Years pass; Kael becomes one of the continent's strongest warriors, turning countless campaigns.
  2. Enemy armies begin retreating at the sight of his banner; soldiers gain courage at the sound of his name.
  3. The name "The Deathbringer (the Death God)" spreads across the continent: his appearance means the enemy's end.
  4. A detail: as he walks through a battlefield, even his own soldiers step aside.
- Key lines: (an enemy command) "On sight of the red banner: retreat. No — run."
- Chapter twist: The third name is born — "the Death God."
- Foreshadow/callback: "Death God" is the name players/enemies use; his wife always calls him Kael (Vol V).
- Writing notes: Write the cold texture of "fear as a weapon."

### Chapter 4 At Last, Recognition

- One line: He gets everything he wanted since childhood, and is still lonely.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Wealth, glory, fame, respect — the world that once rejected him now praises him.
  2. When he was small: "you are a calamity." Now: "you are a hero."
  3. At the banquet: nobles toast him — "without you we would have failed long ago" — but no one asks "do you still want to keep fighting?"
  4. Kael realizes for the first time: they love the "Death God," not "Kael."
- Key lines: Kael: "If I put down my sword tomorrow, who would still know me?" No one answers.
- Chapter twist: The book's most ironic moment — the dream comes true; the heart is emptier.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol V's wife, "I call you Kael," is the only cure.
- Writing notes: Contrast the banquet's clamor with the dead silence inside.

### Chapter 5 War Becomes His Dependency

- One line: Only in war does he know who he is.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael begins actively seeking war — not out of love of killing, but because in peacetime he doesn't know what to do.
  2. Peacetime: he sits in an empty room, sword on his knee, not knowing tomorrow.
  3. He thinks of the knight: the knight left war to find a life; he, leaving war, only has emptiness left.
  4. He has the sword, but no life.
- Key lines: Kael: "I'm good at war. When the war ends, I don't know where to put myself."
- Chapter twist: War changes from "means" to "mode of existence."
- Foreshadow/callback: In Vol V he must relearn "living" — because he has never learned it.
- Writing notes: Write the texture of "emptiness" in daily life — dull and precise.

### Chapter 6 Erian's Descendants

- One line: "He didn't betray himself."
- Scene expansion:
  1. Years later, Kael happens to encounter Erian's family — an ordinary farm household. Erian has grown old or died.
  2. The descendants tell of the old man: "In the end he always said that that day, he hadn't lost." "Grandfather wasn't a hero. He feared death, and was a coward; he ran his whole life. Only that once, he said, he forgot to run."
  3. Kael: "He lost to a god." Descendant: "But he didn't betray himself."
  4. This sentence stabs Kael: he has won countless wars, yet may have been losing all along.
- Key lines: Descendant: "He lost to a god. But he didn't betray himself."
- Chapter twist: Kael dimly sees another definition of "victory" for the first time — not winning, but not losing yourself.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol VII's self-naming of "The Condemned" — not betraying himself.
- Writing notes: A short chapter, one strike to the heart. An ordinary farmyard, an ordinary old man's last words — the more ordinary, the more it cuts Kael.

### Chapter 7 The God Appears Again

- One line: Not a plan, not a challenge — just a crossing of fates.
- Scene expansion:
  1. At some battlefield ruin, Kael meets the War God by chance.
  2. The god is surprised: this human is still alive.
  3. God: "Still chasing proof?" Kael: "This time, I'll make you admit it."
  4. This sentence exposes his true aim — he never left the examination hall of "proof."
  5. The god suddenly says: "In all my life, I have remembered only one mortal." Kael holds his breath — He goes on: "That man's hand holding the sword was trembling. He didn't win. But when he walked past me, I didn't stop him." Kael freezes: he was never remembered by the god, and the man the god remembers — he knows him.
- Key lines: God: "Still chasing proof?" / God: "I have remembered a mortal. It wasn't you."
- Chapter twist: Fate offers a second chance — he uses it to keep making mistakes. The contrast foreshadow pays off: the War God's only remembered man is not Kael (the strongest, who doesn't know why he fights), but Erian (the weakest, who dared to stand against Him).
- Foreshadow/callback: "Still chasing proof?" echoes Vol VII's "You only need to believe in yourself" — two questions, one failure, one (barely) success.
- Writing notes: The reunion must carry the "weight of fate." Kael spent his life begging to be seen; in this moment a god tells him to his face: He remembers someone else.

### Chapter 8 The True Reason for the Second Challenge

- One line: He isn't doing it for the world, not for glory — he just cannot accept the self that failed.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael prepares: he must win, because "if even you won't acknowledge my worth, then I still haven't proved myself."
  2. The reader is clear: his opponent was never the god — it was the abandoned child he used to be.
  3. He practices sword all night; in his dreams are the sound of clansmen's footsteps retreating.
- Key lines: Kael: "I've won every war. I'm only missing this one of yours."
- Chapter twist: He stakes his entire life on one "wrong duel."
- Foreshadow/callback: In Vol VII he asks himself "if I could choose again, would I still take the sword" — the same question, a different answer.
- Writing notes: A pre-battle psychology chapter, entirely interior drama.

### Chapter 9 The Second Duel

- One line: The first time he fought like a child; the second time, like a monster.
- Scene expansion:
  1. The duel begins: shaking heaven and earth. The god takes it seriously for the first time.
  2. God: "You've finally grown strong." — the sentence he most wanted to hear.
  3. Kael finds: hearing it, he feels no joy.
  4. The fight escalates; both sides begin to take wounds.
- Key lines: God: "You've finally grown strong." (Kael: ...I should be happy.)
- Chapter twist: The hollowness of a wish fulfilled becomes one of the book's most painful moments.
- Foreshadow/callback: The destination of "growing strong" is "feeling nothing" — echoing the Vol IV theme sentence.
- Writing notes: Write the combat with "epic feel," but the focus is always on Kael's face.

### Chapter 10 Severing the God's Arm

- One line: A human wounds a god — then he does something that decides his fate.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael finds an opening and severs the god's right arm with one stroke. The moment a legend is born.
  2. But he doesn't stop: he needs to prove he is stronger than the god.
  3. He speaks the insulting words: "Now do you still dare to say my sword isn't strong enough?"
  4. The god looks at him — not with anger in His eyes, but disappointment.
- Key lines: Kael: "Now do you still dare to say my sword isn't strong enough?"
- Chapter twist: The moment of victory is the beginning of defeat.
- Foreshadow/callback: "Restraint" (the knight's first lesson in Vol II) is utterly discarded at this moment.
- Writing notes: This stroke must be written "too fast, too cold" — no glory, only proof.

### Chapter 11 The God's Wrath

- One line: The god is not angry at the defeat, but that he did not understand.
- Scene expansion:
  1. The god doesn't rage; He only looks at him calmly: "You won one battle, and still don't know why you swing your sword."
  2. This is the last reminder. Kael refuses to understand.
  3. The god raises His hand; the curse descends.
- Key lines: God: "You won one battle, and still don't know why you swing your sword."
- Chapter twist: The book's most critical question, decades away from its final answer (Vol VII).
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol VI's "you always choose war" is the unfolding of the curse; Vol VII's "if I must" is the answer.
- Writing notes: Restrained, slow, solemn — a god's wrath is not screamed at the top of His lungs.

### Chapter 12 The Immortal Body

- One line: "You will never have death again. No matter how many wars, how much blood, you must keep walking on."
- Scene expansion:
  1. The curse: eternal life. Not a blessing — a curse.
  2. Kael laughs — he misreads it: "The god has finally acknowledged my power." ("You were afraid you couldn't kill me.")
  3. The god doesn't answer. His silence weighs heavier than the curse.
  4. Kael first feels his body change: wounds heal; time stops moving.
- Key lines: Kael: "You were afraid you couldn't kill me." God (silence).
- Chapter twist: The moment of gaining "eternity" is the moment of losing the right to "end."
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol VII's "what the god gave him was not life, but making him face loss forever."
- Writing notes: After the great laughter, a stretch of silence — make the reader fear for him.

### Chapter 13 The Curse of War

- One line: Wherever he goes, war appears.
- Scene expansion:
  1. From then on, wherever Kael goes, conflict breaks out.
  2. He thinks it is the god's power; the truth: his fame, choices, and methods of resolution attract war.
  3. He is used to resolving conflict with force, so conflict keeps expanding.
  4. He still hasn't noticed "he himself is the cause."
- Key lines: Kael: "This is the god's curse." (Narrator: This isn't magic. This is causality.)
- Chapter twist: The "self-fulfilling" of the curse begins to operate.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol VI's "maybe it's not that war follows me, but that I keep walking toward war" — the truth is revealed.
- Writing notes: Use several small scenes (three towns) to build up the terror of "war follows."

### Chapter 14 The Death God Era

- One line: Everyone knows his name; no one knows Kael.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Decades: Kael becomes a legend. People tell of a warrior who cannot die.
  2. He wins countless victories, accumulates wealth, holds glory.
  3. The heart grows emptier: "proving one deserves to exist" has lost meaning — because everyone knows the name; no one knows the man.
  4. He begins to stare into space in empty places, recalling a vague childhood.
- Key lines: Kael: "My name is Kael." (Tavern keeper: "Kael? That Dead— oh, great Death God!") — He no longer corrects it.
- Chapter twist: The identity is completely swallowed by "Death God."
- Foreshadow/callback: The "name" theme unfolds fully — groundwork for Vol VII's self-naming of "The Condemned."
- Writing notes: Use a time montage: ten years, twenty years, thirty years.

### Chapter 15 The Hollow Victory

- One line: He has won everything, yet doesn't know what to do after victory.
- Scene expansion:
  1. A great war ends; the enemy surrenders; everyone cheers.
  2. Kael stands on the mound of corpses. Someone asks: "Great Death God, where do you go next?"
  3. He is silent — he doesn't know.
  4. That night, he counts the scars on his body: each one corresponds to a "victory."
- Key lines: Kael: "Next..." (He has no answer.)
- Chapter twist: The highest point of the book's "hollowness."
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol V's "the blank after war" directly continues.
- Writing notes: The silence on the corpse mound is one of the book's most famous images; write it thoroughly.

### Chapter 16 The Seed of an Ordinary Life

- One line: "If I stop fighting, who can I still be?" — The first time he is afraid of this question.
- Scene expansion:
  1. After the war, Kael passes a village being rebuilt: children playing, old men repairing houses, lovers leaning together.
  2. He stands far away, watching for a long time.
  3. A thought rises in his heart: "If I stop fighting, who can I still be?"
  4. He has no answer, and he is afraid — this fear shares its origin with the childhood fear of "being abandoned."
- Key lines: Kael (inwardly): "They don't need me. They... don't need me."
- Chapter twist: End of Vol IV — the door to ordinary life cracks open.
- Foreshadow/callback: The opening of Vol V — he walks toward that door.
- Writing notes: The imagery is warm, and inside the warmth there is grief.

## VI. Volume V: Man Who Forgot the Sword (40+ ~ decades)

### Volume Overview

- Time: after the Death God era, from about 40 years old, over decades
- Theme: a legend tries to become a person again
- Volume theme sentence: "He once spent a lifetime becoming a warrior who never lost. In the end, he only wanted to become a father who never let his family down. And that was the first victory he truly won."
- End-of-volume state: He gains love, family, acceptance, an ordinary life; but the past still exists. Greatest error: believing "leaving is protection."

### Chapter 1 The Emptiness After War

- One line: His life becomes a loop: war, victory, glory, emptiness.
- Scene expansion:
  1. The Death God era lasts years; Kael grows weary: every victory is the same — enemies fear, allies worship, kings reward, then the next war.
  2. He suddenly realizes the loop. He stops — the first time he "dares not go on."
  3. He begins to think often of the knight's "come back" and Erian's "still have time."
- Key lines: Kael: "I've been winning for so many years — what exactly am I waiting for?"
- Chapter twist: Weariness becomes the starting point of change.
- Foreshadow/callback: A natural extension of Vol IV's "hollow victory."
- Writing notes: Write the suffocation of the loop — the same banquet, the same toasts, the same next war.

### Chapter 2 The Message Erian Left

- One line: A young man who doesn't know him speaks the old man's final answer for him.
- Scene expansion:
  1. On the road, Kael meets one of Erian's descendants — an ordinary young man who doesn't know the Death God.
  2. The young man talks about his grandfather: "My grandfather feared death more than anything all his life. He always said he'd lived this long only by running fast." A pause. "But he also said one thing: 'In my whole life, I was brave only once. Once was enough.'"
  3. Kael: "Did your grandfather challenge a god?" Young man: "He never told us it was a challenge. He said that day, he forgot to run."
  4. Kael understands for the first time: Erian was not a failure — he didn't win something; he "didn't run" something.
- Key lines: Young man: "He said, that day, he forgot to run."
- Chapter twist: Belated understanding — the words of his two mentors finally "get in."
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol II's knight "knowing who he is after putting down the sword" — only now does it connect. The echo of the contrast foreshadow: what the god remembered was never the strongest man.
- Writing notes: The dialogue looks like small talk, but it is one of the book's emotional hubs.

### Chapter 3 He Admits Defeat for the First Time

- One line: He asks his sword: "Did you really bring me an answer?"
- Scene expansion:
  1. Night, by the campfire. Kael sits alone, looking at the sword that has accompanied him for decades.
  2. He speaks: "Did you really bring me an answer?" No response.
  3. He admits for the first time: perhaps the god was right — he won every battle and didn't know why he fought.
  4. He lays the sword across his knee, feeling it strange for the first time.
- Key lines: Kael: "Did you really bring me an answer?" (Silence.) "...So you won't answer after all."
- Chapter twist: The book's first true "self-negation."
- Foreshadow/callback: This sword's ending (drawn again in Vol VI to protect the village).
- Writing notes: Campfire, monologue, a long night — one of the book's most emotionally deep nights.

### Chapter 4 Putting Down the Sword

- One line: This isn't enlightenment; he is simply tired.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Another war ends. Someone asks: "Death God, where do you go next?"
  2. Kael doesn't answer. He leaves — no announcement, no farewell, no destroying the legend.
  3. "The Death God" continues to exist in stories; Kael disappears.
  4. He discards his recognizable armor and keeps the sword (packed in his luggage, never drawn again).
- Key lines: Kael (one last look back at the battlefield): "I'm not that name anymore."
- Chapter twist: Deliberate disappearance — one of the book's most important actions.
- Foreshadow/callback: "Disappearing" contrasts with Vol VI's "leaving" — this time it's self-rescue; next time it's a mistake.
- Writing notes: The ritual of leaving must be minimal — a back, a road.

### Chapter 5 Seeking an Ordinary Life

- One line: "I've killed dragons." "But can you fix a roof?"
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael arrives at a remote village (Oakwood Village 【tentative】), where no one knows him.
  2. He asks to stay: villagers ask "can you fix houses?" Kael: "I've killed dragons." "But can you fix a roof?" Silence.
  3. He discovers for the first time: killing gods doesn't solve life's problems.
  4. He settles down, renting a small hut. His neighbors are curious and polite.
- Key lines: Villager: "But can you fix a roof?" Kael: "...No."
- Chapter twist: The legend "fails" in front of life.
- Foreshadow/callback: The volume's comic tone is set from here — comedy wrapping tragedy.
- Writing notes: The humor must be cold, as cold as Kael's silence.

### Chapter 6 He Relearns Ordinary Things

- One line: Chopping wood, cooking, haggling — life teaches him to be human.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Chopping wood: he reaches for his sword; a villager says "you don't chop wood with a sword." "Why?" "Because that's a sword." He discovers for the first time: a priceless weapon can't chop wood.
  2. Cooking: he's eaten the best army food, yet doesn't know when to add salt. He burns three pots.
  3. Haggling: a merchant says "this is a fair price"; he says "I once won a city," the merchant: "So?" He: "...I don't know."
  4. Slowly, the legend is pulled back into human shape.
- Key lines: Kael (to the burnt pot): "Not even a god has made me this miserable."
- Chapter twist: Clumsy daily life begins to heal him.
- Foreshadow/callback: "A sword isn't for splitting wood" — the formal groundwork for Chapter 14's "the sword and the firewood."
- Writing notes: Three life vignettes, one section each, light rhythm.

### Chapter 7 The Wife Appears

- One line: What she first saw was a clumsy man who couldn't fix a fence.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Mira 【tentative】: the village seamstress, gentle, direct, not a follower.
  2. First meeting: Kael is fixing a fence (clumsily); she passes by, stops to correct him, and laughs.
  3. She asks: "What did you do before?" Kael pauses: "Traveled." — The first time he doesn't lie, he just doesn't tell the whole.
  4. She doesn't pry. It's the easiest conversation Kael has had in decades.
- Key lines: Mira: "What did you do before?" Kael: "Traveled." Mira: "Then you've been to a lot of places?" Kael: "...Been to a lot of battlefields."
- Chapter twist: The first person who "doesn't need his power" appears.
- Foreshadow/callback: She will become the guardian of the name "Kael."
- Writing notes: The romance must be slow, like a seed sprouting.

### Chapter 8 She Sees the Real Him

- One line: "Does it hurt?" — The first time someone cares about his wound, not what it means.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Villagers whisper about his mark: "That man isn't normal."
  2. Mira isn't afraid. She points at his mark and asks: "Does it hurt?"
  3. Kael: "It hurt long ago." "And now?" "Forgot."
  4. Mira: "Let me pull your sleeve down. Don't catch cold." — not pity, just ordinariness.
  5. Kael is silent for a long time.
- Key lines: Mira: "Does it hurt?" Kael: "It hurt long ago." Mira: "And now?" Kael: "...Forgot."
- Chapter twist: One of the book's gentlest chapters — he is "seen" for the first time, not "defined."
- Foreshadow/callback: The way Vol I's mother soothed him was exactly the same — the old road of "being loved" reappears.
- Writing notes: Keep the dialogue as simple as possible; the stress falls on Kael's "forgot."

### Chapter 9 Marriage and a New Name

- One line: She doesn't call him Death God, doesn't call him hero — she calls him Kael.
- Scene expansion:
  1. The two marry: a small village ceremony, no VIPs, no toasts.
  2. Mira calls him "Kael" — a name no one has truly called in decades.
  3. The first time he hears "Kael" and turns around, instead of walking on.
  4. She mends his clothes, keeps a lamp lit for him, airs his quilts — "daily life" becomes his salvation.
- Key lines: Mira: "Kael, dinner's ready." (He freezes, then says: "...Okay.")
- Chapter twist: The name returns — "Kael" is reactivated.
- Foreshadow/callback: In Vol VII, when Neow asks "your name," he answers "the Condemned" — because by then no one calls him Kael anymore.
- Writing notes: The wedding written small; the life written fine.

### Chapter 10 Becoming a Father

- One line: He can face gods, but doesn't know what to do with a baby.
- Scene expansion:
  1. The child is born (Eilin 【tentative】, a daughter).
  2. Kael is afraid for the first time — not of enemies, but of failing to protect his child.
  3. The baby cries: he flounders. Mira laughs: "You've faced armies, yet you fear a child?"
  4. Kael: "An army tells me how to attack. He doesn't."
  5. He admits for the first time that he is not omnipotent.
- Key lines: Kael: "An army tells me how to attack. He doesn't."
- Chapter twist: Fatherhood becomes his classroom for re-understanding "power."
- Foreshadow/callback: "Protection" has a concrete object for the first time since Vol II.
- Writing notes: Write the image of a "god" floundering under a baby's torment, with comic warmth.

### Chapter 11 The Father's Clumsiness

- One line: He teaches the child swordsmanship, then secretly buys the child sweets.
- Scene expansion:
  1. The child grows; Kael teaches her basic swordsmanship — but not harshly: corrects her stance, then buys sweets.
  2. The child asks: "Father, did you really defeat a god before?" "Mm." "Then you must be amazing."
  3. Kael: "Actually... now I think the more amazing thing is burning the food and still getting you to eat it all."
  4. He begins to learn jokes.
- Key lines: Kael: "I now think burning the food and still getting you to eat it all is the more amazing thing."
- Chapter twist: The legend learns "humor" — the recovery of humanness.
- Foreshadow/callback: The child's "defeated a god" in her eyes contrasts with Vol VI's child helping enemy wounded.
- Writing notes: Sweets, burnt food, laughter — the concrete shape of happiness.

### Chapter 12 The Child Learns the Sword

- One line: "The sword isn't for winning." "Then what is it for?" "Protection."
- Scene expansion:
  1. The child is obsessed with the sword. Kael hesitates: the sword took his whole life.
  2. But he cannot forbid it — because the knight never forbade him.
  3. He teaches her, and tells her: "The sword isn't for winning." She asks: "Then what is it for?"
  4. Kael falls silent. Decades later, he finally knows the answer: "Protection."
- Key lines: Kael: "The sword isn't for winning... It's for protection."
- Chapter twist: The knight's teaching of Vol II, after a lifetime, is finally passed on by Kael.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol VI's child "uses the sword differently" — she learned "protection."
- Writing notes: The sword-teaching scene forms a parallel montage with the Vol II training scene (at the writing level).

### Chapter 13 The Legend Becomes a Joke

- One line: Before, he longed for people to believe his legend; now he turns the legend into a joke.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Drinking in the village, someone asks: "Heard you were pretty good before?" "Not bad." "How good?" "About... better than I am at splitting wood."
  2. Everyone laughs. Kael laughs too.
  3. Late at night at home, Mira asks why he doesn't tell them he's the Death God; Kael: "If I told them, I wouldn't be me anymore."
- Key lines: Kael: "If I told them, I wouldn't be me anymore."
- Chapter twist: He actively chooses "ordinariness" for the first time — half of the growth is complete.
- Foreshadow/callback: In contrast to Vol IV's "they love the Death God" — he is now loved as an ordinary man.
- Writing notes: The misalignment of laughter and truth, handled lightly.

### Chapter 14 The Sword and the Firewood

- One line: "Enemies don't run. Firewood does."
- Scene expansion:
  1. The child sees her father splitting firewood with the longsword (unsuccessfully) and asks: "Why not use it?" "Because it's too long."
  2. Child: "But you beat a lot of people with it." "Enemies don't run."
  3. A pause. "Firewood does."
  4. The child bursts out laughing. Kael laughs too. But the reader knows: this is an old man reflecting on a lifetime.
  5. The laughter dies, and Kael murmurs a name: "Adrian Vale." The daughter asks: "Who is he?" Kael answers in a daze: "A knight who taught me how to use a sword."
- Key lines: Kael: "Enemies don't run. Firewood does." / Kael: "Adrian Vale — a knight who taught me how to use a sword."
- Chapter twist: The book's most famous "gentle joke" — the meaning of power is redefined.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol II's knight "what truly matters isn't the sword" — blooms here. Kael's involuntary utterance of "Adrian Vale" echoes across decades to the Vol II Ch 14 true-name reveal — he never forgot the name, he just didn't know it himself. ## VII. Volume VI: Man Who Lost Home (The Man Who Returned Too Late)

### Volume Overview

- Time: after ordinary life ends, war arrives until home is destroyed
- Theme: "If I fail to protect, can I ever forgive myself?"
- Volume theme sentence: "He once feared he would destroy what he loved. So he chose to leave. Only at the end did he understand: sometimes, leaving is the greatest harm."
- End-of-volume state: He loses family, home, and the illusions of the past; he gains true understanding.

### Chapter 1 The Crack in Peace

- One line: News of war comes from afar; he believes war will not come here.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Years pass; Kael has become part of the village: father, husband, neighbor.
  2. Distant news continues: kingdom conflicts, border unrest, refugee movements.
  3. Villagers discuss "war getting closer." Kael hears it but isn't afraid — he believes he has left the past behind.
  4. The irony is set: he thinks that as long as he doesn't take up the sword, war won't come.
- Key lines: Kael: "That's a distant matter." (Narrator: War never considers any place distant.)
- Chapter twist: The calm begins to crack.
- Foreshadow/callback: The truth of Vol IV's "war follows him" is revealed in this volume — it's not that he seeks war; war comes because of him.
- Writing notes: Alternate daily details with distant news, building "the night before the storm."

### Chapter 2 The Past Comes Knocking

- One line: A refugee recognizes his mark: "You... you're still alive?"
- Scene expansion:
  1. A column of refugees arrives at the village, bringing news of war spreading.
  2. A refugee sees Kael's mark and his face changes: "You... you're still alive?"
  3. The man doesn't recognize "Kael"; he recognizes "the legend": "The Death God."
  4. For the first time the whole village learns who their neighbor is. Mira isn't afraid — she only looks at him, understanding how many stories he has hidden.
- Key lines: Refugee: "The Death God." (The village falls silent.)
- Chapter twist: Identity exposed — "the past" first breaks into "the present."
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol V's "if I told them, I wouldn't be me anymore" — at this moment "I" is renamed.
- Writing notes: The exposure scene must be "slow" — one word that makes everyone turn.

### Chapter 3 Kael's First Deliberate Choice to Avoid

- One line: In the past he drew his sword at problems; now he chooses to hide.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael denies it: "That's all in the past."
  2. Mira asks: "Do you regret it?" Kael: "I don't know."
  3. He begins to avoid the villagers' eyes, avoiding the word "Death God."
  4. He has learned to live without the sword, but not yet to face the past.
- Key lines: Mira: "Do you regret it?" Kael: "I don't know." (He truly doesn't know.)
- Chapter twist: Avoidance becomes the core action of Vol VI.
- Foreshadow/callback: The knight's "avoidance" flaw of Vol II — the generational replication is complete.
- Writing notes: Write the silence beginning to appear inside the "home."

### Chapter 4 War Approaches

- One line: He trains the villagers in defense, yet still refuses to pick up that sword.
- Scene expansion:
  1. The situation worsens; conflict erupts nearby. The village is still calm.
  2. Kael organizes defense: training villagers, planning escape routes, stockpiling supplies.
  3. Someone asks "why are you so good at this"; Kael smiles: "Did some dangerous work before."
  4. He no longer boasts, but he doesn't fully confess either.
- Key lines: Kael (to the villagers): "Remember: surviving matters more than winning."
- Chapter twist: He replaces "fighting" with "protecting" — the practice of the knight's way.
- Foreshadow/callback: This continues Vol VII's "not for victory, but to end the cycle."
- Writing notes: Write the training scenes as "ordinary people's effort," not a war epic.

### Chapter 5 First Clue to the Curse's Truth

- One line: "Maybe it's not that war follows me, but that I keep walking toward war."
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael begins sorting his memories: over the past decades, every place he left eventually saw conflict.
  2. He always thought it was a curse. Now he doubts: "Is it really just a curse?"
  3. He realizes: wars happened where he had actively intervened.
  4. A terrible thought takes shape: "Maybe it's not that war follows me, but that I keep walking toward war." He cannot accept it.
- Key lines: Kael: "Maybe it's not that war follows me. It's that I keep walking toward war."
- Chapter twist: The first piece of the truth — the reader understands that this explains everything.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol IV's "the curse of war" — the curse was only a fable; causality is the truth.
- Writing notes: A psychological deduction chapter; the horror is cold and calm.

### Chapter 6 The Last Family Time

- One line: This should be the warmest chapter of the book — because there won't be another.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Under the shadow of war, the family's daily life: fixing the roof, cooking, teaching the child to read.
  2. The child asks: "Father, what were you like when you were young?" "Troublesome." "How troublesome?" "The kind of troublesome who thought he had to save everyone." "And now?" "Now I just want to fix the roof."
  3. Night: Mira mends clothes by the lamp; Kael polishes an axe beside her. The child sleeps between them.
  4. Kael thinks: if only time could stop at this moment.
- Key lines: Kael: "Now I just want to fix the roof."
- Chapter twist: The peak of happiness = the starting point of tragedy.
- Foreshadow/callback: The memories in Vol VII's ruins — every image of this chapter will reappear at the end.
- Writing notes: Write the poetry of "daily life"; every detail is preparing for the farewell.

### Chapter 7 War Arrives

- One line: First news, then refugees, then soldiers, and finally fire.
- Scene expansion:
  1. News → refugees → soldiers → fire. War approaches the way "real war" does.
  2. At first Kael refuses to fight — he fears becoming the Death God again.
  3. But when the villagers need protection, he picks up the sword.
  4. For the first time everyone sees the real Kael.
- Key lines: Kael (gripping the sword): "...I'm going to become that name again."
- Chapter twist: The sword is drawn again — "the Death God" returns.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol V's gentle "sword and firewood" is burned away by Vol VI's fire.
- Writing notes: The approach of war must be written in four stages, with escalating rhythm.

### Chapter 8 He Becomes the Death God Again

- One line: The enemy recognizes the legend and trembles; he chooses to protect civilians rather than slaughter.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael goes to the front: the enemy sees the red mark and flees in terror.

  3. He finally understands the knight's words — but the war has already begun.

- Chapter twist: He practices "protection," but the world gives him no time.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol II's knight "the sword can protect one person" — he did it; "the sword cannot create peace" — the war remains.
- Writing notes: The reverse writing of battlefield heroism — the hero who protects civilians.

### Chapter 9 The Child's Choice

- One line: The child is becoming who he should have been when young.

  2. Kael sees it: she has what he lost — "the sword isn't for winning" truly practiced.

- Foreshadow/callback: The seed of the next chapter's "make others believe in you" is planted here.

### Chapter 10 The Last Father-Daughter Conversation

- One line: "There's something stronger than the sword." "What?" "Making others believe in you."

### Chapter 11 The Father-Daughter Duel

## VIII. Volume VII: The Last Bargain

### Volume Overview

- Time: after home is destroyed, until the bargain with Neow
- Theme: "A man who spent a lifetime seeking power must finally decide: is he willing to take up the sword again?"
- End-of-volume state: The Condemned is born — he cannot change the past, but he can bear the future.

### Chapter 1 The Man Without a Place

- One line: He has eternal life, but no reason to live.
- Scene expansion:
  1. After home is destroyed, Kael walks aimlessly.
  2. In the past he left home to grow strong, left battlefields for peace; now he leaves everything only because he has nowhere to go.
  3. He finally understands what the god gave him was not life, but "facing loss forever."
  4. He tries to die: jumping off cliffs, joining battles — wounds heal; life continues.
- Key lines: Kael: "So the cruelest part of not dying is that you don't even have the right to give up."
- Chapter twist: Despair becomes absolute — creating space for the bargain with Neow.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol IV's "eternal life" curse shows its full weight in this moment.
- Writing notes: Write the daily routine of "wanting to die and failing," calm, precise, despairing.

### Chapter 2 Traces of the Blight

- One line: War comes from madness; the dead cannot rest.
- Scene expansion:
  1. On his travels Kael sees the world changing: soldiers attacking comrades, villagers destroying their own homes, the dead not resting.
  2. A new disaster: the Blight — the infected lose reason, their bodies mutate, and they eventually become monsters.
  3. Kael first faces "an enemy the sword cannot solve": he can kill monsters, but cannot stop the disaster.
  4. He remembers the god's words: "Your sword is sharp. But your heart is too loud."
- Key lines: Kael: "The sword can kill monsters. But not what makes monsters."
- Chapter twist: The sword's limit is presented head-on for the first time.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol III's god's prophecy-like judgment finally comes due.
- Writing notes: The Blight scenes must write "horror as daily life" — not monsters, but people becoming monsters.

### Chapter 3 Searching for Answers

- One line: "If even a god cannot solve it, why should I keep fighting?"
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael begins investigating the Blight's origin: survivors, scholars, temple ruins.
  2. Discovery: the Blight is not a natural disaster; it comes from a higher power; even gods cannot escape it.
  3. Rumor: a god has gone mad from the Blight.
  4. For the first time Kael asks "why" — not "how to be stronger."
- Key lines: Kael: "If even a god cannot solve it, why should I keep fighting?"
- Chapter twist: For the first time in the book he asks "why" rather than "how to win" — growth complete.
- Foreshadow/callback: The absence of "why" in Vol III and its presence in Vol VII.
- Writing notes: Make the investigation like a detective novel, peeling back layer by layer.

### Chapter 4 Neow's Gaze

- One line: An appraising gaze, from an existence that has been watching him for a long time.
- Scene expansion:
  1. After the Blight spreads, Kael feels watched — not hostile, not blessed; appraising.
  2. This existence knows his past, his curse, his name.
  3. Kael is wary, but not hostile — he no longer has the strength for hostility.
  4. Image: in the gloom, a blue outline (Neow) appears for the first time (in the distance).
- Key lines: (Kael) "Come out. You've been watching me a long time." (No answer, but the air changes.)
- Chapter twist: A fourth party's gaze — the story is about to enter the bargain.
- Foreshadow/callback: Neow has been "watching" since Chapter 1 — in the game she does the same.
- Writing notes: A suspense chapter; Neow does not appear, only "the gaze."

### Chapter 5 First Meeting

- One line: "Do you still want to die?"
- Scene expansion:
  1. A waste land, the sky gray; Kael sits alone in the ruins.
  2. A voice appears: "Do you still want to die?" Kael looks up: Neow, blue-skinned.
  3. Kael: "Who are you?" Neow: "One who can give you answers."
  4. Kael sneers: "I've heard too many promises like that."
  5. He is no longer the easily trusting child of his youth — deceived by gods, war, and himself.
- Key lines: Neow: "Do you still want to die?" Kael: "I want to. But I can't die."
- Chapter twist: Neow speaks for the first time — probing, appraising, to the point.
- Foreshadow/callback: The book's opening "The Condemned" and this "man seeking death" — exactly the game's starting state.
- Writing notes: Dialogue tension: two beings who trust neither each other nor anyone.

### Chapter 6 Neow Makes the Offer

- One line: "I need a warrior who cannot die."
- Scene expansion:
  1. Neow doesn't deceive — at least on the surface: the Blight is devouring the world.
  2. She needs someone to enter the Spire and find the source — where a power that can change everything lies.
  3. Kael: "You need a warrior." Neow: "No. I need a warrior who cannot die."
  4. Kael falls silent: she knows his curse.
- Key lines: Neow: "I need a warrior who cannot die."
- Chapter twist: His curse becomes "needed capital" for the first time.
- Foreshadow/callback: Irony — the reason he wanted all his life to be accepted; this time it is "immortality."
- Writing notes: The first clause of the bargain, cold and honest.

### Chapter 7 The Lie About the Curse

- One line: She tells the truth — but not all of it.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Neow says she can lift his curse, on condition that he destroys the Blight's source.
  2. Kael: "Why me?" Neow: "Because others would die."
  3. This sentence is true, but incomplete: she needs him because immortality makes him the perfect tool.
  4. The reader learns (suspense retained): Neow is not a savior; she is part of the disaster.
- Key lines: Neow: "Because others would die." (A pause.) "You wouldn't."
- Chapter twist: The contract takes shape — the reader sees the trap; Kael sees it too (but has no choice).
- Foreshadow/callback: Game setting — Neow's relationship with the Spire and corruption.
- Writing notes: The boundary between lie and truth must be subtle — she didn't lie; she told only half.

### Chapter 8 Kael's Doubt

- One line: "You only need to believe in yourself." — These are the most dangerous words.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael doesn't accept immediately: "You are a god. I believed in gods once. They gave me a curse."
  2. Neow is silent — she knows.
  3. Kael: "Why should I believe you?" Neow: "You don't need to believe me. You only need to believe in yourself."
  4. This sentence strikes the greatest weakness of his life — "believing in himself" is exactly what he never learned.
- Key lines: Neow: "You only need to believe in yourself."
- Chapter twist: The only "psychological breakthrough point" in the bargain — she precisely finds his wound.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol II's knight "accept yourself" — Neow turns the same sentence into bait.
- Writing notes: This is the chapter showing Neow's "intelligence" — she isn't a villain; she is a negotiator.

### Chapter 9 Neow and Kael's Similarity

- One line: What she sees is a failure — but she admires him.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Neow looks at Kael: not a hero, a failure. But she admires him.
  2. Because they are alike: both have made mistakes, both want to fix the past.
  3. The difference: Neow tries to use others to fix her errors; Kael has finally learned to bear his own.
  4. This is also the seed of their future conflict (later in the game plot).
- Key lines: Neow: "We both want to rewrite the past. The difference is, I leave the cost to others. You choose to carry it yourself."
- Chapter twist: The mutual understanding of two "fixers" — a brief moment of humanity.
- Foreshadow/callback: Groundwork for the in-game truth that "she is not a savior."
- Writing notes: Neow should carry the aura of a "merciful merchant."

### Chapter 10 The Last Look at the Past

- One line: "If I could choose again, would I still take the sword?" — "If I must."
- Scene expansion:
  1. Before accepting the contract, Kael returns to the ruins of home, carrying his wife's necklace.
  2. He remembers: the child, the wife, the knight, Erian.
  3. He asks himself: "If I could choose again, would I still take the sword?"
  4. The Kael of the past would say "yes"; now he is silent for a long time: "If I must."
  5. The difference: he no longer loves the sword, but he is willing to bear responsibility.
- Key lines: Kael: "If I must." (Wind blows through the ruins.)
- Chapter twist: The book's final choice on its theme — "taking up the sword" changes from proving to bearing.
- Foreshadow/callback: Vol I's "wait for me to return" — he has returned, in another way.
- Writing notes: Ruins + necklace + memory montage — the book's emotional closing chapter.

### Chapter 11 Accepting the Contract

- One line: "Defeat the Spire, end the Blight, and I will lift your curse."
- Scene expansion:
  1. Neow extends her hand — not a command, a bargain.
  2. Terms: defeat the Spire, end the Blight, lift the curse.
  3. Kael: "And if I fail?" Neow: "You will begin again. Your life will not end."
  4. Kael murmurs: "Immortality again." He understands: it is a curse, but also a chance.
- Key lines: Kael: "Immortality again." (He smiles — a bitter smile.)
- Chapter twist: The contract is signed — the prelude to the game's start.
- Foreshadow/callback: The death mechanic of the game — "you will begin again."
- Writing notes: The ritual of the handshake/touch; the color contrast of blue and red.

### Chapter 12 Why He Accepts

- One line: Not for glory, proof, or heroes — he finally has a way to repay the past.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Kael asks and answers himself: not for glory, proof, or becoming a hero.
  2. But: "I destroyed many things. If there's anything left to save, let me try once."
  3. He puts the necklace into his breast and grips it.
  4. For the first time he acts not for "being accepted," but for "repayment."
- Key lines: Kael: "I destroyed many things. If there's anything left to save, let me try once."
- Chapter twist: The book's final shift of motive — from "proving himself" to "bearing responsibility."
- Foreshadow/callback: The endpoint of the six-stage character arc.
- Writing notes: A monologue chapter, calm and steadfast.

### Chapter 13 The Condemned Is Born

- One line: "The Condemned" is not his punishment; it is his redemption.
- Scene expansion:
  1. Neow: "Your name?" Kael is silent: "Names no longer have meaning."
  2. Neow: "Then how do you wish to be called?"
  3. He looks at the mark on his hand, at his scarred body: "The Condemned."
  4. Not self-mockery, not glory — acceptance. He is not a hero, a Death God, or a calamity; he is a man bearing his guilt.
- Key lines: Kael: "The Condemned."
- Chapter twist: Self-naming complete — he defines himself for the first time.
- Foreshadow/callback: The title and the in-game character name converge here; Vol I's naming of "calamity" is formally overturned.
- Writing notes: Quiet and solemn — this is a "birth," not a "surrender."

### Chapter 14 Entering the Spire

- One line: "No. But I have been preparing for too long."
- Scene expansion:
  1. The Spire appears: towering, twisted, full of Blight.
  2. Kael grips his sword — for the first time in decades, not to prove himself, but to end the cycle the sword brought.
  3. Neow: "Ready?" Kael: "No." A pause. "But I have been preparing for too long."
  4. He steps into the Spire. The story ends — the game begins.
- Key lines: Kael: "No. But I have been preparing for too long."
- Chapter twist: The final chapter — the junction between text and game.
- Foreshadow/callback: The game's opening screen — The Condemned standing before the Spire.
- Writing notes: The ending shouldn't be sentimental; it should have a "sense of departure" — like opening a door.

### 4. Volume Theme Sentences (per the original setting)

- I: "When he was born, people feared his power. So he spent a life proving he deserved power. Only later did he understand: what people truly feared was never power."
- II: "The knight gave him a sword. He thought it was the answer. But what the knight truly wanted to give him was: knowing who he is after putting the sword down."
- III: "He heard everyone tell him the sword cannot bring peace. But he only heard one sentence: your sword is still not strong enough."
- IV: "He spent a life proving he was not a calamity. In the end, everyone believed he was a hero. Only he himself knew he was nearly forgetting how to be a person."

- VI: "He once feared he would destroy what he loved. So he chose to leave. Only at the end did he understand: sometimes, leaving is the greatest harm."
